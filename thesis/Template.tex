\addchap*{Abstract}
% Draft 2026-10-31. Numbers from the final train/validation split (HANDOFF.md,
% Sec. 6-8). Re-check against Chapter 5 once it is written.
Manual assembly is still largely done by hand, and knowing which step a worker
is performing can support training, quality assurance and process analysis.
Wrist-worn inertial sensors are a common basis for such activity recognition,
but many assembly activities lead to similar wrist movements while producing
clearly different sounds. This thesis investigates whether combining inertial
and acoustic data from a single wrist-worn device improves the recognition of
manual assembly activities, which fusion strategy is best suited for this, and
whether the resulting models can run on the device itself.

A dataset of nine activity classes was recorded with M5StickC Plus2 devices in
six sessions with two participants, resulting in 2565 paired two-second windows
of inertial and audio data. Single-modality convolutional neural networks, a
random forest on hand-crafted features, and three fusion strategies were
compared on a grouped train/validation split that prevents overlap between
training and validation windows. The fusion strategies combine the two
modalities at the input level, at the level of learned embeddings, and at the
level of class probabilities. Reduced versions of the three fusion models were
quantised to 8-bit integers and deployed with TensorFlow Lite Micro on the
ESP32-based wearable.

Fusion improved the recognition clearly over the inertial model alone, which
reached 87.7\,\% accuracy, and over the audio model alone. The best result was
achieved by a small early-fusion model with 93.2\,\% accuracy and a macro-F1
score of 0.92, followed by decision-level fusion with class-specific weights
and embedding-level fusion at about 90\,\%. On the device, all three fusion
variants ran within the available memory, with an inference time of about 1.1
to 1.4\,s per two-second window, and their quantised outputs matched the
reference implementation. The results show that audio is a valuable addition
to wrist-worn inertial sensing for assembly activities and that multimodal
fusion is feasible on a low-cost microcontroller. They are, however, based on
a small dataset from two participants and do not yet show how well the models
generalise to new persons.
\\\\
\textbf{Keywords: human activity recognition, multimodal sensor fusion,
wearable sensors, manual assembly, TinyML}
