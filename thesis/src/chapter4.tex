This chapter describes the models used for activity recognition and the
procedure used to train and evaluate them. It begins with an overview of the
experimental design and its relation to the research questions. It then covers
the input representations derived from the inertial and acoustic windows of
Chapter~\ref{ch:data}, the single-modality models, the three fusion strategies,
and a classical baseline using hand-crafted features. It closes with the training
procedure, the deployment of selected models on the wearable device and the
evaluation protocol that all models share. The results obtained with these models
are presented in Chapter~\ref{ch:results}.

\section{Overview of the Experimental Design}
\label{sec:design}

The experiments compare models that differ in one respect at a time. All models
are trained and evaluated on the same windows and the same train/validation
split, and they use the same input normalisation and the same training settings.
Differences in accuracy can therefore be attributed to the model design and not
to differences in the data or in the training procedure.

The models fall into four groups, each linked to one or more of the research
questions formulated in Chapter~1:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Single-modality models} use either inertial or acoustic data
  alone. They form the reference point against which the benefit of adding audio
  is measured (RQ1, RQ3).
  \item \textbf{Fusion models} combine both modalities at different stages of
  processing. They are combined at the input level (early fusion), at the level of learned
  representations (embedding-level fusion), or at the level of class predictions
  (decision-level fusion). Their comparison addresses RQ2.
  \item \textbf{A classical baseline} uses conventional machine learning without
  deep neural networks. Simple statistical features are computed by hand from
  each window and classified with a random forest. Comparing the neural networks
  with this baseline shows how much is gained by learning the features from the
  data instead of defining them in advance (RQ3).
  \item \textbf{Reduced model variants} with fewer parameters were designed with
  the memory and computational limits of the target device in mind. They connect
  the offline comparison to the deployment on the device described in
  Section~\ref{sec:deployment} (RQ4).
\end{itemize}

The choice of model types follows from two requirements of this thesis. The
models must recognise activities from short windows of sensor data, and the best
of them must later run on a microcontroller with very limited memory and
computing power. Convolutional neural networks (CNNs) meet both requirements.
They learn characteristic local patterns directly from the signal, such as the
shape of a hammer strike in the acceleration or the frequency content of a
screwdriver in the spectrogram, and they need comparatively few parameters
because the same small filters are applied across the whole window. For these
reasons, CNNs are among the most widely used architectures in wearable activity
recognition and in embedded audio classification {[}CITATION{]}. Recurrent
layers, which process a sequence step by step, can capture the temporal order of
a signal more explicitly but are more expensive to execute {[}CITATION{]}. They
are therefore included only as an optional pooling stage of the audio model, so
that their benefit can be measured against their cost. The three fusion
strategies cover the three stages at which modalities are commonly combined in
multimodal learning {[}CITATION{]}. The random forest, finally, is a standard
reference method in activity recognition that performs well on small datasets
and requires little tuning {[}CITATION{]}.

All experiments use the dominant-wrist dataset described in
Chapter~\ref{ch:data}, with 2565 paired windows across nine activity classes. The
only exception is the standalone audio model, which additionally uses audio
windows from the non-dominant wrist and the waist. The reason for this is given
in Section~\ref{sec:audio-model}.

\section{Input Representations}
\label{sec:inputs}

\subsection{Inertial Windows}
\label{sec:imu-input}

Each inertial window holds 160 samples of the six channels (three-axis
acceleration and three-axis angular velocity) recorded at 80\,Hz. Before entering
a model, the window is resampled to 200 time steps by linear interpolation along
the time axis, so that all inertial models take inputs of size $200 \times 6$.
The target length of 200 was chosen to match the time resolution of the audio
spectrogram, which, as described in Section~\ref{sec:logmel}, has 198 frames per
window. This allows the early fusion model presented in
Section~\ref{sec:early-fusion} to place both modalities on one time axis without
coarsening the audio representation. Using the same length in all inertial models
also keeps their inputs identical across the experiments. Missing or non-finite
values, which can remain at the very edges of a recording after decimation, are
replaced by zero.

Accelerometer and gyroscope channels have different physical units and value
ranges. Each channel is therefore standardised to zero mean and unit variance.
The mean and standard deviation of each channel are computed on the training
split only and then applied unchanged to the validation data, so that no
statistics of the validation set enter the training process. As described in
Section~\ref{sec:deployment}, the same statistics are later embedded in the
device firmware and applied to the live sensor data.

\subsection{Audio Log-Mel Spectrograms}
\label{sec:logmel}

Raw audio windows of 32\,000 samples are too long to be used directly as input
to a small model. Each window is therefore converted into a log-mel spectrogram,
a time-frequency representation that is widely used for sound classification
{[}CITATION{]}. The conversion consists of three steps. First, a short-time
Fourier transform (STFT) splits the signal into overlapping frames of 25\,ms (400
samples), shifted by 10\,ms (160 samples), and computes the frequency spectrum of
each frame with an FFT length of 512. Second, the magnitude spectrum of each frame
is projected onto 40 frequency bands between 20\,Hz and 8\,kHz. The bands are
spaced according to the mel scale, which resolves low frequencies more finely than
high ones, similar to human hearing {[}CITATION{]}. Third, the natural logarithm of
the band energies is taken. A small constant of $10^{-6}$ is added beforehand so
that silent bands do not produce the logarithm of zero.

A two-second window produces a spectrogram of 198 frames $\times$ 40 mel bands.
This reduces the input size by roughly a factor of four compared with the raw
waveform, while keeping the spectral and temporal structure that distinguishes,
for example, the continuous whine of a powered screwdriver from the short,
broadband impacts of hammering. As with the inertial data, the spectrogram is
standardised per mel band using the mean and standard deviation of the training
split. Figure~\ref{fig:logmel} summarises the feature extraction, and
Table~\ref{tab:inputs} lists the resulting input representations.

\section{Single-Modality Models}
\label{sec:single}

\subsection{Inertial Model}
\label{sec:imu-model}

The inertial model is a one-dimensional convolutional neural network (1D CNN)
that convolves along the time axis of the window. 1D CNNs are a common choice for
wearable activity recognition because they learn local motion patterns directly
from the raw signal and remain small enough for embedded use {[}CITATION{]}.

The network has two convolutional blocks. The first block applies 64 filters with
a kernel size of 5, followed by batch normalisation and max pooling with a factor
of 2. The second block applies 128 filters with a kernel size of 3, followed by
batch normalisation. Global average pooling then reduces the temporal dimension
to a single feature vector. This vector passes through a dense layer with 64
units and through dropout with a rate of 0.3, before a softmax output layer with
one unit per class. The output of the 64-unit layer also serves as the embedding
for the embedding-level fusion described in Section~\ref{sec:embedding-fusion}.

\subsection{Audio Model and Temporal Pooling Variants}
\label{sec:audio-model}

The audio model treats the log-mel spectrogram as a single-channel image and
processes it with a two-dimensional CNN {[}CITATION{]}. It has two convolutional
blocks with 32 and 64 filters of size $3 \times 3$, each followed by batch
normalisation and $2 \times 2$ max pooling. The resulting feature map has a size
of $49 \times 10 \times 64$ (time $\times$ frequency $\times$ channels).

How this feature map is reduced to a single vector determines which properties of
the sound the classifier can use. Three variants were therefore evaluated, which
are shown in Figure~\ref{fig:audio-variants}.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Global average pooling (GAP).} The feature map is averaged over
  both time and frequency {[}CITATION{]}. This is the standard approach and gives a compact model. However,
  averaging over time removes the information about how the sound develops within
  the window. Two activities with a similar average spectrum but a different
  temporal pattern, such as the regular strikes of a hammer and the steady sound
  of a screwdriver, can then become hard to separate.
  \item \textbf{Recurrent pooling (GRU).} The feature map is reshaped into a
  sequence of 49 time steps with 640 features each and passed through a
  bidirectional gated recurrent unit (GRU) with 32 units per direction
  {[}CITATION{]}. The GRU summarises the sequence while keeping its
  temporal order, so the model can use rhythm and envelope information.
  \item \textbf{Hybrid pooling.} The GAP vector and the GRU output are computed in
  parallel and concatenated. The GAP vector describes the average timbre of the
  window, and the GRU output describes its temporal structure. The classifier can
  then rely on whichever information is useful for a given class.
\end{itemize}

In all three variants, the pooled vector passes through a dense layer with 64
units and dropout with a rate of 0.4, followed by the softmax output.

Because audio content depends less on where the sensor is worn than inertial
data does, the standalone audio model is trained on audio windows from the
dominant wrist, the non-dominant wrist and the waist. This roughly
triples the amount of acoustic training data. The pooled audio is used only for
the standalone audio model. All fusion models use dominant-wrist audio
exclusively, so that every audio window is paired with the inertial window from
the same device and time interval.

The pooling variants are compared on the standalone audio model. Within the
fusion models, the acoustic component uses the GAP variant. With 23\,945
parameters it is about six times smaller than the recurrent variants, and it
avoids recurrent layers, whose sequential computation is costly on the target
microcontroller. This keeps all fusion models within reach of the deployment
described in Section~\ref{sec:deployment}. The performance of the three variants is

\subsection{Early Fusion}
\label{sec:early-fusion}

Early fusion combines the two modalities before any learning takes place, so
that a single network learns from both signals jointly. Two forms were
implemented.

\textbf{Channel concatenation.} The log-mel spectrogram ($198 \times 40$) is
resampled along its time axis to 200 frames, matching the length of the inertial
window. The two inputs are then concatenated along the feature axis, giving a
$200 \times 46$ input with 6 inertial and 40 acoustic channels. Each of the 46
channels is standardised with its own training statistics. This step is
particularly important here, because the channels of the fused input have very
different value ranges. In the recorded data, the acceleration lies roughly
between $-1.5$ and $+1.5$\,g and the angular velocity between $-200$ and
$+200\,^\circ$/s, while the log-mel values lie roughly between $-14$ and $+3$. A
convolutional filter sums weighted values from all channels. Without
standardisation, the gyroscope channels would therefore dominate these sums
simply because of their larger numbers, and the network would first have to learn
to compensate for the different units before it could learn from the content of
the signals. After standardisation, every channel has zero mean and unit variance
and starts with the same influence on the result. The fused input is then
processed by a 1D CNN with the same structure as the inertial model described in
Section~\ref{sec:imu-model}.

\textbf{Unified spectrogram.} Here the inertial data are also converted into
spectrograms, so that both modalities share one image-like representation. A
short-time Fourier transform with a frame length of 20 samples, a hop of 2
samples and an FFT length of 32 is applied to each of the six inertial axes. The
log magnitudes are resized to the $198 \times 40$ grid of the audio spectrogram
by bilinear interpolation. The six inertial spectrograms and the audio
spectrogram are stacked as the seven channels of a single input and processed by
the 2D audio CNN with global average pooling.

\subsection{Embedding-Level Fusion}
\label{sec:embedding-fusion}

Embedding-level fusion first trains the inertial and the acoustic model
separately, as described in Section~\ref{sec:single}. Their weights are then
frozen, and for each window the outputs of the 64-unit dense layer are taken as
embeddings. The two 64-dimensional embeddings are concatenated into a
128-dimensional vector, which is the input to a small fusion head. The head
consists of a dense layer with 64 units, dropout with a rate of 0.4 and a softmax
output, with 8841 parameters in total.
Only the fusion head is trained in this second stage. It can learn how the two
modalities relate to each other without changing the feature extraction that each
backbone has already learned.

\subsection{Decision-Level Fusion}
\label{sec:decision-fusion}

Decision-level fusion combines the predictions of the two independently trained
models {[}CITATION{]}. Both models end in a softmax layer, which outputs one value
between 0 and 1 for each of the nine classes, with all nine values summing to 1.
These values can be read as the confidence of the model that the window belongs to
the respective class. For a window $x$, let $p^{\mathrm{IMU}}_c(x)$ denote the
probability that the inertial model assigns to class $c$, and
$p^{\mathrm{audio}}_c(x)$ the probability that the acoustic model assigns to the
same class. The fused score of class $c$ is the weighted average of the two,
\begin{equation}
  p_c(x) = w \cdot p^{\mathrm{IMU}}_c(x) + (1 - w) \cdot p^{\mathrm{audio}}_c(x),
  \qquad w \in [0, 1].
  \label{eq:global-fusion}
\end{equation}
The weight $w$ determines how much the inertial model is trusted relative to the
acoustic model. With $w = 1$, only the inertial model is used, with $w = 0$, only
the acoustic model is used, and with $w = 0.5$, both count equally. The fused
score is computed for every class, and the class with the highest score is the
prediction. The weight is selected by trying the 21 values $0, 0.05, \dots, 1$ and
keeping the one that gives the highest accuracy on the validation data. Compared
with a trained fusion head, this method has only a single free parameter and is
therefore less prone to overfitting on a small dataset.

A single weight, however, assumes that the relative reliability of the two
modalities is the same for every class. There is little reason to expect this for
the present activity set, because the activities differ strongly in how much
characteristic motion and how much characteristic sound they produce. For some
classes, the inertial model is therefore likely to be the more reliable source,
and for others the acoustic model. Which modality is more reliable for which class
is not fixed in advance but determined from the data. The method was therefore
extended to one weight per class. With $C = 9$ classes, this gives
nine weights $w_1, \dots, w_9$, and the fused score of class $c$ becomes
\begin{equation}
  p_c(x) = w_c \cdot p^{\mathrm{IMU}}_c(x) + (1 - w_c) \cdot p^{\mathrm{audio}}_c(x).
  \label{eq:perclass-fusion}
\end{equation}
The only difference to Equation~\ref{eq:global-fusion} is that the weight now
carries the index $c$. When the fused model computes the score of a particular
class, it uses the weight of that class, and it can therefore rely on whichever
sensor is more informative for that class. A value of $w_c$ close to 1 means that
the score of class $c$ is determined mainly by the inertial model, and a value
close to 0 means that it is determined mainly by the acoustic model.

A short example illustrates the effect. Consider a window of the class
\texttt{measuring}, for which the two models produce the probabilities shown in
Table~\ref{tab:fusion-example}. For readability, only three classes are shown. The
inertial model recognises the activity, whereas the acoustic model hears almost
silence and leans towards \texttt{idle}. With a global weight of $w = 0.5$, the
correct class wins only by a small margin. If the weight of \texttt{measuring} is
raised to $w_{\mathrm{measuring}} = 0.9$, its score is taken almost entirely from
the more reliable inertial model, and the margin increases clearly. The score of
\texttt{idle} does not change, because its own weight has not changed.

It remains to find suitable values for the nine weights. For the single weight of
Equation~\ref{eq:global-fusion}, all 21 candidate values could simply be tried.
For nine weights, trying every combination would require $21^9$, or about
800 billion, evaluations, which is not practical. The weights are therefore
adjusted one at a time, while all others are kept fixed. This simple strategy is
known as coordinate ascent {[}CITATION{]}. The name refers to the fact that only
one coordinate of the weight vector, that is, one class weight, is changed in
each step, and that a change is kept only if it raises the accuracy. The search
proceeds as follows.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item All nine weights start at the best single weight found for
  Equation~\ref{eq:global-fusion}. The search therefore begins with the result of
  global fusion and can only improve on it.
  \item The weight of the first class is set in turn to each of the 21 values
  $0, 0.05, \dots, 1$, while the other eight weights stay unchanged. For each
  value, all validation windows are classified with
  Equation~\ref{eq:perclass-fusion}, and the accuracy is computed. The value with
  the highest accuracy is kept, provided that it is better than the accuracy
  before this step.
  \item Step~2 is repeated for the second class, then for the third class, and so
  on, until each of the nine weights has been adjusted once.
  \item Since a change in one weight can make a different value of an earlier
  weight slightly better, steps~2 and~3 are carried out a second time.
\end{enumerate}

In total, the search therefore evaluates $2 \times 9 \times 21 = 378$ weight
settings, which takes less than a second. A second round is sufficient because
the weights hardly influence each other. Changing the weight of one class only
affects windows for which this class and another class received almost the same
score. For most windows, one class is clearly ahead, and changing a single weight
does not alter the prediction. The resulting weights show, for each class, how
much the fused model relies on the inertial and how much on the acoustic model.
They are therefore also used to interpret the results in
Chapter~\ref{ch:results}. Figure~\ref{fig:perclass-fusion} summarises the
procedure.

\section{Deployment-Oriented Model Variants}
\label{sec:small-models}

The models described so far were designed for offline evaluation. On the target
microcontroller, however, inference time and memory usage are strict limits.
The TensorFlow Lite Micro build for this platform executes convolutions with
unaccelerated reference kernels, so their cost grows directly with the number of
filters and with the size of the feature maps. This is explained in more detail in
Section~\ref{sec:platform-sw}. Reduced variants were therefore created that
keep the overall architecture but use fewer filters.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Small early fusion model.} The structure of the
  channel-concatenation model with 32 and 64 instead of 64 and 128 filters. This
  reduces the number of multiply-accumulate operations, and with it the inference
  time, by roughly a factor of four.
  \item \textbf{Small inertial backbone.} A 1D CNN with 32 and 64 filters.
  \item \textbf{Small audio backbone.} A 2D CNN with 16 and 32 filters. Its
  convolutions use strides of 4 and 2 instead of separate pooling layers, so the
  downsampling happens within the convolution itself. As a result, a feature map
  at the full $198 \times 40$ resolution never has to be held in memory, which
  would otherwise take up a large part of the device's working memory.
\end{itemize}

The small backbones are used for the small embedding-level and decision-level
fusion models. To reduce overfitting, three regularisation measures were added to
them {[}CITATION{]}. L2 weight decay with a factor of $10^{-4}$ is applied to all
learned layers, the dropout rates are raised to 0.4 and 0.5, and the loss function
uses label smoothing of 0.1.

In addition, the training data of the small backbones are augmented. One
augmented copy is generated for each training window, so that the training set
doubles in size. The validation data are never augmented.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Inertial windows} receive a small random three-dimensional rotation
  of up to $\pm 15^\circ$, applied jointly to the accelerometer and gyroscope axes
  to mimic small changes in how the device sits on the wrist, together with
  per-channel scaling (factor 0.9--1.1), additive noise (3\,\% of the channel's
  standard deviation) and a mild time warp (speed factor 0.9--1.1)
  {[}CITATION{]}.
  \item \textbf{Spectrograms} are shifted in time by up to $\pm 10$ frames, with
  the frames that leave one end of the window re-entering at the other. In
  addition, random parts of the spectrogram are masked, following the SpecAugment
  method originally proposed for speech recognition {[}CITATION{]}. Two blocks of
  up to 20 consecutive frames and two blocks of up to 6 adjacent mel bands are
  overwritten with the mean value of the spectrogram. Because the hidden parts
  change from copy to copy, the model cannot depend on a single short moment or a
  single frequency range and has to use the information of the whole spectrogram.
\end{itemize}

\section{Training Procedure}
\label{sec:training}

\subsection{Software and Hardware Environment}
\label{sec:environment}

All models were implemented in Python~3.10 with TensorFlow~2.10 and Keras
{[}CITATION{]}. Training ran on an NVIDIA GeForce
RTX~4070 Laptop GPU through the DirectML backend for Windows. This backend
supports TensorFlow only up to version~2.10, which fixed the framework version for
the whole project. Two parts of the pipeline could not run on the GPU with this
backend and had to be adapted. The short-time Fourier transform used for the
spectrograms was moved to the CPU, and the fused recurrent kernel of the GRU layer
was replaced by an unrolled implementation. Both changes affect only where and how
the computation is executed, not its result. Data preparation used NumPy, pandas
and librosa {[}CITATION{]}, and the classical baseline
used scikit-learn {[}CITATION{]}.

\subsection{Training Settings}
\label{sec:settings}

All neural networks were trained with the settings listed in
Table~\ref{tab:training}. The class imbalance described in
Section~\ref{sec:stats} is handled through class weights in the loss function.
The weight of each class is inversely proportional to its frequency in the
training split, so that errors on rare classes count more than errors on frequent
ones {[}CITATION{]}. The training data themselves are left unchanged, without
oversampling or undersampling.

The backbones and the early-fusion models are trained for a fixed 150 epochs at a
constant learning rate. On a small validation set, the validation accuracy can
fluctuate noticeably from one epoch to the next, so the weights at the end of
training are not necessarily the best ones. After training, each model is
therefore set back to the weights of the epoch with the highest validation
accuracy.

In the Keras version used here, the standard early-stopping callback restores the
best weights only when it actually stops training early. If training runs the full
number of epochs, the weights of the last epoch are kept without any warning. A
small custom callback was therefore written that stores the best weights during
training and restores them at the end. The effect of this choice is examined in
Chapter~\ref{ch:results}. Because the best epoch is selected on the validation
data, the procedure introduces a slight optimistic bias into the reported results,
a point taken up in the discussion of limitations in
Chapter~\ref{ch:discussion}.

\section{Deployment on the Wearable Device}
\label{sec:deployment}

The models described so far were trained and evaluated on a desktop computer. To
answer RQ4, selected models were also brought onto the M5StickC Plus2, the same
device that recorded the training data. Two conditions must hold for this to
succeed. The model has to fit into the memory of the microcontroller and run fast
enough for continuous use. In addition, the device has to transform the live
sensor signals in exactly the same way as the training pipeline did, because even
a correct model produces wrong predictions if its input differs from what it saw
during training. This section describes how both conditions were addressed. The
measured memory use, inference time and on-device accuracy are reported in
Chapter~\ref{ch:results}.

\subsection{Target Platform and Software Stack}
\label{sec:platform-sw}

As described in Section~\ref{sec:platform}, the device is built around the
ESP32-PICO-V3-02, a dual-core processor clocked at up to 240\,MHz with 520\,KB of
on-chip SRAM. Only part of this memory is available to the application, because
the Arduino framework and the device libraries reserve their own share. The
firmware was developed with PlatformIO and the Arduino framework. Neural network
inference uses TensorFlow Lite for Microcontrollers {[}CITATION{]}, a version of
TensorFlow Lite designed for devices without an operating system and without
dynamic memory allocation. The FFT for the audio features is computed with the
arduinoFFT library {[}CITATION{]}.

The TensorFlow Lite Micro port available for this platform does not include the
optimised neural network kernels that the chip manufacturer provides for newer
processors. All layers are therefore executed with the generic reference
implementations. This is the reason why the inference time grows directly with
the number of filters and the size of the feature maps, and why the reduced model
variants described in Section~\ref{sec:small-models} were developed.

Three firmware variants were implemented, one for each fusion strategy. They share
the same capture and preprocessing code but differ in the number of networks they
run, as summarised in Table~\ref{tab:firmware-variants}. Each variant is a separate
PlatformIO build environment, so that the data-collection firmware of
Chapter~\ref{ch:data} and the three inference variants can be flashed
independently of each other.

\subsection{Model Conversion and Integer Quantization}
\label{sec:quantization}

The trained Keras models store their weights as 32-bit floating-point numbers. For
the microcontroller they are converted into the TensorFlow Lite format and
quantized to 8-bit integers {[}CITATION{]}. In this form, each value is
represented by an integer $q$ between $-128$ and $127$, and the original real
value $r$ is recovered as
\begin{equation}
  r = s \cdot (q - z),
  \label{eq:quantization}
\end{equation}
where the scale $s$ and the zero point $z$ are fixed for each tensor. Quantization
reduces the size of the weights by a factor of four. It also allows all
computations to be carried out in integer arithmetic, which is the form best
supported by TensorFlow Lite Micro on microcontrollers.

Full integer quantization was used, so that not only the weights but also the
intermediate results and the input and output of each network are 8-bit integers.
To choose suitable scales for the intermediate results, the converter needs to
know which value ranges occur in practice. It therefore receives a representative
dataset of 200 standardised windows from the training split, which it passes
through the network to record the range of every intermediate tensor. The
validation data are not used in this step.

The conversion is carried out by an export script. Besides the converted model,
it writes all values that the firmware needs to reproduce the preprocessing into
C header files, which are listed in Table~\ref{tab:firmware-contents}. The
firmware is then compiled together with these files. Because all constants are
produced automatically from the same objects that were used during training,
errors caused by copying values by hand are ruled out.
Figure~\ref{fig:export-pipeline} shows this process.

\subsection{On-Device Signal Processing}
\label{sec:device-preprocessing}

The preprocessing described in Chapter~\ref{ch:data} and
Section~\ref{sec:inputs} was originally implemented in Python and applied offline
to complete recordings. On the device, the same steps have to be carried out on a
single two-second window, in real time and with little memory.

\textbf{Inertial data.} During data collection, the IMU delivered about 250
samples per second, which were reduced to 80\,Hz by averaging consecutive samples,
as described in Section~\ref{sec:decimation}. The firmware repeats this step. It
collects all IMU samples that arrive during the two-second window and averages
them in 160 consecutive groups, which corresponds to the 160 samples of a training
window. The result is then resampled to 200 time steps by linear interpolation,
standardised with the stored training statistics and quantized with the input
scale of the network. To save memory, the last three steps are combined into a
single pass that writes the result directly into the input tensor of the network.

\textbf{Audio data.} A two-second audio window consists of 32\,000 samples. Stored
as 16-bit integers, it would occupy 64\,KB, which would not fit into memory
together with the networks. The log-mel spectrogram is therefore computed while
the audio is being recorded. The firmware reads the microphone in chunks of 160
samples, which corresponds to the 10\,ms hop of the STFT, and keeps only the most
recent 400 samples, which form the current frame. For each new frame, it
multiplies the samples by the Hann window, computes the FFT with a length of 512,
applies the mel filter bank and takes the logarithm. Only the resulting
$198 \times 40$ mel values are kept. The samples are scaled to the range from $-1$
to $1$ beforehand, as in the training pipeline. For the early fusion model, the
spectrogram is then resampled to 200 frames and written into the audio channels
of the fused input. The decision-level and embedding-level variants pass the
$198 \times 40$ spectrogram to the audio network unchanged.

Two details of the training pipeline are easy to overlook and were reproduced
explicitly. First, the STFT in TensorFlow applies a Hann window to each frame by
default, even though the training code does not request it. Second, the mel
filter bank of TensorFlow differs slightly from those of other libraries. As
listed in Table~\ref{tab:firmware-contents}, both the window and the filter bank
are therefore taken directly from TensorFlow and embedded as tables instead of
being recomputed on the device.

\subsection{Firmware Structure and Inference Loop}
\label{sec:firmware-loop}

After start-up, the firmware initialises the display, the microphone and the
networks and runs the self-test described in Section~\ref{sec:device-verification}.
It then enters a loop, shown in Figure~\ref{fig:inference-loop}, that repeats the
following steps.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item A two-second window is recorded. The IMU is read between successive audio
  chunks, so that both modalities cover the same period of time, as in the
  synchronised training data.
  \item The inertial and acoustic inputs are prepared as described in
  Section~\ref{sec:device-preprocessing}.
  \item The network or networks are executed.
  \item For decision-level fusion, the output probabilities of both networks are
  converted back to real values with Equation~\ref{eq:quantization} and combined
  with the per-class weights of Equation~\ref{eq:perclass-fusion}. For
  embedding-level fusion, the two embeddings are passed to the fusion head.
  \item The predicted class and its confidence are shown on the display and sent
  over the serial port, together with the time required by each step.
\end{enumerate}

Recording and inference take place one after the other. While a window is being
processed, no new data are recorded, so consecutive windows do not overlap as they
do in the training data. A new prediction is therefore available every two seconds
plus the processing time.

All memory is reserved statically when the firmware is compiled. TensorFlow Lite
Micro places all intermediate tensors of a network in a fixed memory block, the
tensor arena, whose size has to be chosen in advance. The sizes in
Table~\ref{tab:firmware-variants} were chosen so that all buffers fit into the
available memory together, and the firmware reports at start-up how much of each
arena is actually used. The network weights are not part of the arena, because
they remain in flash memory and are read from there during inference. In
addition, each network registers only the nine operations it actually uses
instead of all available operations, which keeps the program smaller.

\subsection{Verification of the Deployment Pipeline}
\label{sec:device-verification}

When the device produces a wrong prediction, the cause can lie in two places.
Either the network itself does not run correctly on the device, or the input it
receives differs from the training data. To separate these two cases, each
firmware runs a self-test at start-up. It uses one labelled window per class from
the validation split. These windows were standardised and quantized in Python
with the same constants as on the device and are embedded in the firmware. They
are written directly into the input tensor, bypassing the sensors and the
on-device preprocessing, and the device reports how many of them are classified
correctly. If the self-test succeeds but live predictions are poor, the error must
lie in the capture or in the preprocessing.

For the live pipeline, the firmware also prints diagnostic values in every cycle.
These include the number of captured IMU samples, the mean acceleration along the
three axes and the range of the raw audio samples. The mean acceleration shows how
the device is oriented relative to gravity and can be compared with the training
data. This makes it possible to detect problems such as a different wearing
orientation, whose effect is examined in Chapter~\ref{ch:results}.

\section{Evaluation Protocol}
\label{sec:evaluation}

\subsection{Grouped Train/Validation Split}
\label{sec:split}

As described in Section~\ref{sec:windowing}, the windows were created with
50\,\% overlap, so neighbouring windows share half of their samples. A random split would place such
neighbours on both sides of the train/validation boundary. The model would then
be evaluated on data that it had partly seen during training, and the measured
accuracy would overestimate its performance on new data {[}CITATION{]}.

To prevent this, the windows are first grouped into activity instances.
Consecutive windows with the same label belong to the same group as long as their
start times are at most 1.5\,s apart, that is, 1.5 times the window stride.
Consecutive windows of one uninterrupted activity occurrence start exactly 1\,s
apart, so the additional 0.5\,s serves as a margin. Small irregularities in the
window start times therefore do not split one occurrence into several groups,
while occurrences separated by a pause or by another activity, which leave a gap
of at least one further stride, are still kept apart. The 2565 windows form 125 such groups.

Some of these groups are very long. In recordings with a single long activity,
such as walking or carrying a container, one group can contain more than half of
all windows of a class. If whole groups were assigned to one side of the split,
such a group alone would exceed the intended validation share of its class.
Groups with more than 20 windows are therefore cut into blocks of equal size with
at most 20 consecutive windows each, which gives 204 blocks in total. The split is
then made separately for each class. The blocks of a class are shuffled and
considered one after another, and a block is assigned to the validation set only
if this brings the number of validation windows of the class closer to 20\,\%.
The remaining blocks form the training set.

Cutting a group into blocks creates a new boundary inside a continuous activity,
where the last window of one block overlaps the first window of the next. The same
situation occurs at every transition between two activities. For this reason,
every training window whose time span overlaps a validation window is removed
from the training set. Since windows start 1\,s apart and last 2\,s, this affects
exactly the windows directly adjacent to a validation block. In total, 56 windows
(2.2\,\%) are discarded in this way. As a result, no training window shares any
samples with a validation window, and a fixed random seed makes the split
identical for all models, so that their results can be compared directly.
Figure~\ref{fig:grouped-split} illustrates the principle.

\subsection{Metrics}
\label{sec:metrics}

Overall accuracy, the share of correctly classified validation windows, is used as
the main measure for comparing the models. Because the classes are imbalanced,
accuracy alone can hide poor performance on small classes. Precision, recall and
F1-score are therefore also reported for each class, together with the
macro-averaged F1-score, which gives every class the same weight {[}CITATION{]}. Confusion matrices are used to identify which activities are mistaken
for one another. Unless stated otherwise, all results are reported on the
validation set of the grouped split and come from a single training run per
model.

\subsection{On-Device Measurements}
\label{sec:device-measurements}

The deployed models are evaluated in three ways. First, the accuracy of the
quantized models is measured in Python on the full validation set and compared
with that of the original floating-point models, which isolates the effect of
quantization. Second, the firmware reports the size of each model, the used part
of each tensor arena and the time needed for capture, preprocessing and inference,
measured with the system timer of the microcontroller. Third, the self-test and
live tests with the device worn on the wrist show whether the complete pipeline
works under real conditions.

\section{Chapter Summary}
\label{sec:ch4-summary}

This chapter introduced the models compared in this thesis. Inertial windows are
processed as $200 \times 6$ time series by a 1D CNN, and audio windows as
$198 \times 40$ log-mel spectrograms by a 2D CNN, for which three pooling variants
were developed to make use of the temporal structure of the sounds. The two
modalities are combined at the input level (channel concatenation and unified
spectrogram), at the level of learned embeddings, and at the level of class
probabilities, where a per-class weighting reflects the different reliability of
the two modalities across activities. A random forest on hand-crafted features
serves as a classical baseline, and reduced, regularised variants prepare the
deployment on the device. All models share the same input normalisation, class
weighting, training settings, best-epoch selection and grouped train/validation
split. Selected small models are converted to 8-bit integer arithmetic and
executed on the wearable device by firmware that reproduces the complete
preprocessing of the training pipeline and checks its own correctness with
embedded validation windows. Chapter~\ref{ch:results} presents the results of
both the offline and the on-device evaluation.
